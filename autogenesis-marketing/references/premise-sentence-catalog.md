## LOCKED HERO COPY (operator-confirmed 2026-08-09)

The first premise sentence the operator has actually accepted end-to-end. Hero opens with a four-beat rhetorical move (establish common ground → name constraint → pivot → promise) lifted verbatim from the operator's interview. Hero sub uses imperative voice to describe what the PLAYER does (not what the game does). Hero closer answers the implied question "*what happens after I write the move?*" in two imperatives + one PvP-delayed-consequence.

### What the locked copy does that the four variants did not

| Failure mode the variants had | How the locked copy fixes it |
|---|---|
| Described what the game does (third-person) | Describes what the PLAYER does (imperative voice) |
| No goal named | "expand your empire" — a goal |
| No opponents named | "your rival empires" — opponents |
| No stake named | "control of the world" — a stake |
| Closer was three numbers + an abstraction (*"Four players. Twenty-five rounds. One shared world. No two games are ever the same."*) — operator flagged weak | Closer is two imperatives + one PvP-delayed-consequence. No numbers in the closer. Numbers (four players, 25 rounds) move into the body sections where the rule-bound game language belongs. |
| Hero sub didn't land the four concepts together | Hero sub names all four concepts in two sentences: complete control ("anyway you like"), anything can happen ("any action you can think of"), PvP ("rival empires"), no two games alike (implied via "control of the world" — every match produces a different winner) |

### The load-bearing distinction

**Describe-the-game vs describe-the-player.** Premise sentences that describe the game (third-person: "the world writes the story," "the validator checks the play") fail. Premise sentences that describe what the player does (imperative: "create your commander," "take any action") land. The reader needs to be told what THEY will do, not what the system does. The four canonical variants above are all describe-the-game; the locked hero sub is describe-the-player. Any future premise sentence should be tested against this distinction first.

### Numbers vs imperatives in the hero closer

The v4 closer was *"Four players. Twenty-five rounds. One shared world. No two games are ever the same."* — three numbers and an abstraction. Operator verdict: *"This part is weak."* Numbers don't sell because they don't tell the reader what happens. The closer should answer the implied question (*what happens after I write the move?*), not restate the game structure. The four concepts (player count, round structure, shared world, procedural uniqueness) don't all die — they move into the body sections where they belong:
- "Four players" → Section 4 (turn loop): *"Four players take turns..."*
- "Twenty-five rounds" → Section 5: *"Twenty-five rounds. The world remembers every one."*
- "One shared world" → Section 5: *"All four players see everything."*
- "No two games are ever the same" → Section 5 (named systems): karma / nemesis / Elder Gods / procedural prose.

The hero closer does ONE thing: tell the player what happens after they write the move.

### Operator voice works in small doses but breaks down across a full page

Draft F (the 2026-08-09 v3 draft) tried to do the entire page in the operator's interview-transcript cadence. The hero landed — *"Every game has rules. In most games, the rules will limit what you can do. There are clear bound constraints that exist, and nothing you do or try can change that. But not here. Here, the limit is your own imagination."* — but everything after that line, in the body sections, fell apart. Operator verdict: *"Everything up to here in F is strong. This sounds human. This sounds correct. After that it falls apart."*

The lesson: **operator cadence is the front door. Once the reader is inside, the body copy needs a different register.** Each body section can use the four-beat rhetorical move (establish common ground → name constraint → pivot → promise) WITHOUT trying to mimic the operator's single-sentence declarations. Imperative verbs throughout, but structured paragraphs, not operator-cadence one-liners.

## Rejected Phrasings (and Why)

These collapse into one of the two anti-premise failure modes ("AI writes funny stories" or "strategy game with a chat box"). Do not revive them without explicit operator approval.

- "Autogenesis is a unique AI-driven strategy game where..." — leads with adjective stack, hides the loop.
- "Powered by an AI dungeon master..." — leads with "AI dungeon master" before showing the loop; reader classifies as ChatGPT-with-prompts.
- "An endlessly replayable 4-player narrative strategy game." — four adjectives, no verb, no loop.
- "Like AI Dungeon but with a board game." — frames against the wrong reference class.
- "Where imagination becomes a move." — pretty, but the rules promise ("somehow it just works") is invisible.
